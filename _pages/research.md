##### **1. Physics-empowered tactile robots.**
![Physics-empowered tactile robots]({{ '/assets/img/Picture1.jpg' | relative_url }}){: width="900px"}
This project develops a photoelastic, vision-based tactile sensor that directly visualizes contact stress through a reflective photoelastic imaging principle. Unlike conventional tactile sensors, which infer contact information from the surface geometry deformation of the sensing material, this platform provides stress patterns in real time. The sensing mateiral is designed as a fatigue-resistant, color-changing hydrogel engineered with substantial molecular entanglements and hygroscopic salts, enabling it to remain mechanical and optical stable under repeated cyclic and dynamic loading in ambient conditions. Together, these features provide a robust platform for high-resolution tactile sensing and mechanical characterization.

Related works:
- **J. Liu**, W. Li, Y. She, S. Blanchard and S. Lin. 
  [Fatigue‐Resistant Mechanoresponsive Color‐Changing Hydrogels for Vision‐Based Tactile Robots](https://advanced.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/adma.202407925), 
  *Advanced Materials*, 2024

##### **2. Mechano-transport in soft materials.**
![Mechano-transport in soft materials]({{ '/assets/img/picture2.jpg' | relative_url }}){: width="700px" style="display: block; margin: 0 auto;"}
This project explores mechanical deformation as a new design space for controlling particle diffusion in stretchable soft materials. We develop a mechano-diffusion theory to investigate how particle transport changes in deformed hydrogels under uniaxial tension, biaxial tension, and torsion.

Related works:
- **J. Liu** and S. Lin. [Strain-engineered particle diffusion in uniaxially deformed polymer networks](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022509624001984), *Journal of the Mechanics and Physics of Solids*, 2024.

##### **3. Multiscale modeling for network materials.**
![Multiscale modeling for network materials]({{ '/assets/img/Picture3.jpg' | relative_url }}){: width="700px" style="display: block; margin: 0 auto;"}
Entangled networks are ubiquitous in tissues, polymers, and fabrics. However, their mechanics remain insufficiently understood due to the complexity of the topological constraints at the network level. In this project, we develop mesoscale discrete network models, a mathematical framework that models entangled networks as graphs, capturing topological constraints of entanglements. This framework establishes fundamental principles of linking topology to mechanics of entangled networks and offers a foundational tool for designing reconfigurable materials.


Related works:
- J. Huang<sup>†</sup>, **J. Liu<sup>†</sup>**, and S. Lin. [Topological Mechanics of Entangled Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17813), ***arXiv:2509.17813***, 2025.
  <details markdown="1">
  <summary><strong>Comment</strong></summary>
 I consider this my first truly collaborative project, and it is also my first time working closely with a mathematician in an interdisciplinary work. It is also one of the most difficult projects of my Ph.D. I started working on it in the fall of my third year and continued until the winter of my fourth year. The process was exhausting, but it became an incredibly valuable research experience. 
 The idea began during a walk with Dr. Huang after dinner near the MSU Business College. We happened to get into a casual discussion about a recent PRL paper from Dr. Xuanhe Zhao’s group on nonlocal mechanics in spring networks. That casual conversation naturally led us to talk about the hydrogel from my recent Advanced Materials paper.The hydrogel showed remarkable mechanical properties, and people often said that entanglements could redistribute stress. However, there was no quantitative model explaining exactly how that redistribution occurred.
 Dr. Huang is an excellent mathematician. After I described the problem, he immediately recognized that it could be formulated and studied mathematically. That conversation marked the beginning of our collaboration. The project was difficult from the very beginning because many of the concepts, variables, and modeling frameworks had to be defined from scratch. We went back and forth many times, held countless meetings, and repeatedly discussed what the central question should be and how we could approach it. By the time we submitted the paper, I did not want to see the word “entanglement” again for quite a while.
 Still, this was one of the most rewarding experiences of my Ph.D. I learned how to formulate an open-ended scientific question, build a multiscale model step by step, and collaborate with researchers from a very different discipline. It was the hardest project I worked on during my Ph.D., but it was also the one from which I learned the most. (July 2026)
  </details>

- **J. Liu**, S. Lin, and J. Huang. A Discrete HRR-Like Theory for Fracture in Nonlinear Lattice Networks. Submitted, 2026. 
  <details markdown="1">
  <summary><strong>Comment</strong></summary>
  This was probably the most exciting research experience of my Ph.D. It felt like exploring through a fog, with each observation unexpectedly revealing the next step. While working on our previous entangled-network project, we first study about spring-network models and noticed an interesting phenomenon: as the network became larger, its fracture toughness gradually converged to a constant value. This type of size convergence is common in real soft materials, but seeing it emerge in a discrete network model was surprising and not easy to understand. We wanted to figure out why it happened. The breakthrough came from our experimental observations. I first noticed that, under large deformation, the stretch of each individual chain increased linearly with the applied deformation. We then found that, even in much larger networks and at large stretches, different chains had different slopes, but the ratios between those slopes remained constant. Based on this invariant sequence of slopes, Dr. Huang conjectured a mathematical formula. Dr. Lin then realized that the formula was exactly the same as the classical HRR field solutio. That moment was incredible. We started from a simple and somewhat puzzling experimental observation, followed the pattern step by step, and unexpectedly arrived at a results similar to a classical fracture-mechanics theory. It was such a surprising and rewarding research journey—exactly the kind of experience that makes scientific discovery so much fun. (July 2026)
  </details>
